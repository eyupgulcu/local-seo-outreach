# Message patterns

## Why benefit first

An ask that makes the reader do work for you ("test this for two weeks and tell us what is missing") reads
as free labor. In one small field test, that kind of ask drew a quick objection ("why should I spend time on
your test"). The same offer reframed as something that brings the business extra customers got a "yes, let's
try" within minutes. In another case, a recipient who asked for details responded to a concrete description
of how it works and an offer to show it by phone. Both observations are tiny samples (a handful of
replies). Treat them as direction, not proof, and update this note when your own numbers arrive.

Other patterns that held up:

- Corporate phrasing and long paragraphs look like bulk mail on chat apps. Short sentences and one question
  feel human.
- A self-introduction sentence ("I am the founder of...") adds nothing; the signature already says who you
  are.
- Lead with an elbow-grease detail (something you did by hand for them) rather than praise.
- A smaller ask converts better than a bigger one: "may I send a short screen recording" beats "can we book
  a call".

## Structure of a first message

1. Greeting, with the business or person name if known.
2. One sentence on what you noticed, specific and true.
3. The benefit in their terms.
4. The small optional ask, written as a question.
5. Signature: name and business. Nothing after it.

Banned endings: apology, "ignore this if not relevant", "reply STOP", "reply one word and I will not write
again", and any other opt-out line inside the body. If the law needs an opt-out path, use the sending
system's footer or header.

## Examples (invented names)

Email, first contact:

```
Subject: Your product catalog and customers who search by cable type

Hello Example Electric team,

I checked how someone in Sampletown finds an electrical wholesaler online. Your reviews are strong, but the
website has no product catalog, so a customer who searches for a specific cable cannot see that you stock
it. That is lost calls you probably never hear about.

I put the details in a one-page report. May I send it over?

Jane Doe
Example Studio
```

Chat app, shorter:

```
Hello, good day. I looked at Example Electric's website from a customer's point of view and found one thing
that is probably costing you calls. I wrote it up on one page, free. Shall I send it here?
Jane, Example Studio
```

Follow-up, about three days later, new information, still one question:

```
Hello again. One more detail from the check: when I asked an AI assistant for wholesalers in Sampletown it
named two of your competitors and not you. The report shows what they have that your site does not. Would
you like it?

Jane Doe
Example Studio
```

## Follow-up rules

- One follow-up, about three days after the first, with something new in it. No "just checking in".
- If there is no reply after the follow-up, stop. Do not send a third.
- A reply that says no, or a legal opt-out, goes onto the suppression list immediately and permanently.
- When a prospect objects, answer the objection in the same framing ("this is extra work for you" becomes "this
  is extra customers for you"), do not argue.

## Lint checklist

- First sentence is not about you.
- The benefit is named in the reader's terms.
- Exactly one ask, small and optional.
- No percentage, ranking or income claim.
- No apology or opt-out line in the body.
- Last line before the signature is a question.
- Signature is the last thing in the message.
- Every factual statement is reproducible today.
